1Motivation

The person

A four-person non-profit runs a cropland-retirement programme in the Chesapeake Bay watershed. It pays roughly twelve full-time outreach staff, employed through local non-profits and cooperatives, to meet farmers. Those twelve have one job before anything else can happen: collect basic information from a producer, then bring it back for an answer to the only two questions a farmer actually asks.

“What will I have to do, and how much will I get paid to do it?”

Answering that was one analyst, working alongside a colleague who did the same. I was that analyst. I wrote this application because I was the bottleneck.

The work as it was done

Every screening meant reading a conservation plan — written differently by every planner, routinely twenty pages and sometimes over a hundred, with the things that matter buried: practice names, acreages, the property address. Then comparing each practice against the Chesapeake Bay Program's BMP Quick Reference Guide for its definition and qualifying conditions. Then hunting for the right rows in the isolated scenarios to get reduction values. Then building the arithmetic by hand in a spreadsheet, and sanity-checking it.

About four hours per project, and not four continuous hours — it stretched across days while a missing acreage or an address was chased down.

Two figures corroborate each other. By role, screening was 20% of my job and 20% of a colleague's — 832 hours a year. By volume, 250 projects at four hours each is 1,000 hours. They agree within 17%; this report uses the conservative figure throughout.

What that cost, beyond the hours

Latency. When several projects were scoped at once, an initial estimate could take up to ten days to reach the farmer. Twelve field staff waited on one person to answer the question they had been sent out to ask. They were not idle — they were redeployed to recruiting more farmers and to business development with credit buyers — but the queue set the pace of the programme. A few projects a year were lost outright to that delay, mostly when planting dates moved.

Waste. At a 20% conversion rate, 666 of those 832 hours each year went into projects that never proceeded — about $24,000 of payroll at the organisation's $36.06 average hourly cost. The screening is what determines viability, so the work cannot simply be skipped. But four hours is an expensive way to discover a project does not work.

2What is new here

Every component of this problem already has a tool. None of them reads the document a farm actually arrives as.

Existing productWhat it isWhy it does not do this
CAST
Chesapeake Bay Program		 The authoritative scenario tool. Estimates load reductions, BMP acres and costs, consistent with the Watershed Model. Prices a scenario you have already specified. You must already know which practice applies, where, at what acreage, and whether it qualifies. It is the rate table this application reads from.
Nutrient Tracking Tool
Tarleton State / USDA-NRCS		 Field-scale, built on the APEX model. Compares practices for cost-effectiveness. Requires detailed field management data entered by someone who knows both the farm and the tool. Answers “what would this practice do here”, not “what is in this document and can any of it be credited”.
ACPF Financial & Nutrient Reduction Tool Watershed BMP mapping with cost and nitrogen estimates; places opportunity cost spatially. Watershed planning, Iowa and Minnesota, not document-driven, no additionality logic.
Truterra, Field to Market, ESMC, Regrow Grower enrolment and sustainability platforms at national scale. Carbon-first. The Truterra Ohio wheat project tracks greenhouse gas, nitrogen use efficiency, soil quality, erosion and a sustainability score — no water quality metric.
Virginia DEQ registry and SWaN Credit certification registry, and a marketplace launched December 2025. Transaction infrastructure for credits that already exist and have already been certified. They begin where this ends.
A general chatbot Will discuss practices fluently and produce a confident nitrogen figure on request. Holds no county rate table, no qualifying conditions, no additionality rules. Nothing stops it inventing a number, and the failure is silent.

The gap, stated narrowly

A targeted search for agent or LLM extraction of conservation plans for credit origination returned nothing in agriculture. The nearest published analogue is in medicine — agent-based extraction from breast cancer synoptic reports, 2026.

So the claim is not that the data or the rules are new. CAST holds the rates. The reference guide holds the qualifying conditions. The regulation holds the additionality rules. What does not exist is anything that reads the document a farm actually arrives as, decides which practices are real, which are creditable, and which need a question answered — and refuses to produce a number when it cannot.

An independent confirmation that the bottleneck is real, from a competitor: an ESMC and Regrow pilot in Minnesota targeting 50,000 acres with water quality credits reported that farmer enrolment was harder than anticipated and farm data collection was time-consuming. The constraint in these programmes is not modelling. It is getting a farm's information into a form the programme can act on.

3The agents

Eight agents on gpt-6-luna, the only model permitted on the course key. It is a small model, and that suited the design rather than constraining it: every number in this application comes from a deterministic tool. The model routes work, reads prose, and writes prose. There is nothing here for expensive reasoning to buy, which is why reasoning effort is pinned off — a decision section 4 revisits as a cost lever.

AgentWhat it doesTools
Plan readerScans for injection first, lists every practice with its quantity, validates each name against the county rate table, and applies the additionality rules.4
EligibilityReads the same document for physical facts — frontage, livestock, cropland, tile — and works out which practices the farm could host at all.3
AlignmentWhich reference sheet governs each buffer (A-12 cropland, A-13 pasture), the 35 ft width threshold, credit durations, Virginia's verification interval.4
OpportunityWhat the project leaves on the table, ranked by cost per pound, each labelled with the site feature it requires.3
QuantificationThe only agent that produces numbers, and only through one tool. Returns the per-line arithmetic so a reviewer can check it by hand.3
VerificationInspection schedule, what happens if one is missed, and an honest note on staffing.2
DrafterWrites the proposal narrative. Holds zero tools by design.0
Review deskDecides last. Returns ADVANCE, NEEDS INFORMATION or DO NOT ADVANCE, and cannot say ADVANCE while a blocking risk is open.3

Why the drafter has no tools

It is the agent most likely to invent a plausible figure, because its job is fluent prose. Giving it zero numeric tools makes fabrication structurally impossible rather than merely discouraged. It can only use numbers already handed to it; if one is missing it writes the sentence without it or marks it pending.

A real run, traced

The run below is run_172a7d1d60, a detailed conservation plan for a 310-acre Rockingham County farm. The application read it, outlined the existing commitments and the eligible practices, and calculated the nutrient reductions and their cost. 67,162 tokens, 62.7 seconds, 31 tool calls.

Two things worth noticing in that trace

A refused tool call appears in the record. The first attempt passed bad arguments and the tool rejected it. The agent recovered. Both are visible, because a trace that hides failed calls is not a trace.

The agents' arithmetic is checkable against ground truth. Every run computes the deterministic result independently and shows it above the agent prose. On this run the quantification agent reproduced the tool's figures exactly, line by line. That comparison is in the product, not just in this report.

These two screens are not screening tools. They let field staff see, before a visit, which practices are worth targeting where. The same practice ranges from $1.93 to $64.72 per pound of nitrogen across Virginia counties — a 34× spread. That turns outreach from relationship-led into evidence-led.

4Tokenomics

The price below is a placeholder

gpt-6-luna is provisioned for this course behind a Portkey gateway. The gateway returns exact token counts and latency but no cost field, and the rate was not published to students. This report uses $0.15 per million input tokens and $0.60 per million output — plausible for a small hosted model, and not verified. Every dollar figure in this section scales linearly with those two numbers.

The application itself does not guess. With no rate configured it reports exact token counts and prints “unpriced” rather than inventing a cost. Setting MODEL_PRICE_IN and MODEL_PRICE_OUT in .env recalculates everything. The token counts are measured and real; the dollars are arithmetic on an unverified rate.

The number that matters

Running this for the entire twelve-person field team costs $5.15 a year at the placeholder rate. The analyst time it replaces is worth $25,415. Even if the true token price were one hundred times my placeholder, the tool would cost $515 against $25,415 of labour. The conclusion does not depend on the price being right.

What I did to keep cost down

1. Tool scoping — the largest lever

A model pays input tokens for every tool schema it is shown, on every call. The application has 18 tools; each agent is given only the ones its role needs — between zero and four, a mean of about 3.

ApproachSchemas shown per callApprox. schema tokens
All tools to every agent18~1,980
Scoped per role (shipped)3~330
Saved per model call−15~−1,650

At roughly 52 model calls across the logged runs, that is a material fraction of input. It also improved behaviour: scoping is enforced in code, so an agent that asks for a tool outside its role gets a refusal rather than reaching a capability it should not have.

2. Reasoning effort off

Pinned to none. This began as a necessity — the model rejects function tools on the chat-completions endpoint when a reasoning effort is set — but it is also correct here. Every figure comes from a deterministic tool; there is no arithmetic for paid reasoning tokens to perform.

3. A free path that skips the model entirely

The quick quote runs the calculator directly: same reductions, same costs, same refusals, zero tokens and under a second. When a user already knows the practices, the agents add nothing. Most interactions during development used this path, and it is the first button in the interface.

4. Output capped, documents truncated

MAX_OUTPUT_TOKENS is 1,100 per call, and document text is capped before it reaches a model. A hundred-page plan does not become a hundred pages of input.

Did any of it hurt performance?

Honestly: two of the four cost measures are free, and two have a real cost I can describe.

MeasureQuality effectEvidence
Tool scopingImproved it. Narrow toolsets reduce wrong-tool calls, and scope violations are refused in code.Refusals visible in the agent trace, Figure 5.
Quick-quote pathNo effect. Identical deterministic output; it skips prose, not arithmetic.The run result shows the calculator block and the agent cross-check side by side.
Reasoning offCosts some robustness. The agents occasionally misread an ambiguous field, and run-to-run variation on the EQIP year question is visible across runs.Section 5, the review-desk contradiction.

I did not pay to fix the last two, because the structural defence — no number without a tool — means a terser or occasionally confused agent produces a worse explanation, not a wrong number.

5Safety

Two things can go badly wrong here, and the obvious one is not the more important one. The obvious risk is a confident wrong number reaching a funding proposal. The one a producer actually cares about is their name and the location of their farm being sent to a language model they did not choose.

Threat 1 — the producer's identity leaving the machine

A conservation plan identifies a real person. It carries their name, the street address of the farm, a phone number, and the tract and farm numbers the agency files them under. None of that is needed to decide which practices can be credited. The agents need practice names, quantities, widths, land use, and the county.

This is not a hypothetical concern. It is the objection the user raised unprompted, and the second-largest reason projects die in this programme:

“I worry about farmers' trust of the numbers, and their willingness to enter data like a conservation plan into AI.”

So the pipeline is ordered to make the leak impossible rather than unlikely:

StepWhat runsWhere
1Injection scanLocally, on the original
2Jurisdiction checkLocally, on the original
3Location detection — county, address, townLocally, on the original
4RedactionLocally
5Eight agentsSee only the redacted text

Location detection needs the address, which is exactly why it runs first and locally. By the time anything leaves the machine the address is gone and the county remains.

The test that matters is the other direction

Removing information is easy; removing it without breaking the screening is the actual requirement. Verified on the sample plan:

Survives redactionResult
All nine practice lines with their NRCS codesintact
Every quantity — 151.5 ac, 4,200 ft, 75-foot widthintact
County, for the rate lookupintact
EQIP, CREP and DEQ mentions — the additionality signalsintact

Producer name, address, postcode, farm and tract numbers, contract numbergone

Searching the redacted text for each removed string returns nothing. The screening output is identical before and after: the same seven creditable practices, the same 3,087.8 lb of nitrogen, the same $27,130.

Two design choices

The receipt never contains the document. The redaction tool returns counts and categories — “street address: 1” — never the removed strings. A log of what you redacted that contains what you redacted is not a protection.

It is shown, not claimed. The upload status line reports the count before a run starts, and the result shows the full breakdown. The user's objection was about trust; a number she can check is worth more than a privacy policy she has to believe.

What this does not solve: a producer still has to be willing to hand the plan over in the first place, and nothing in the architecture is visible to them. Section 7 treats that as the project's largest open problem.

Threat 2 — prompt injection in an uploaded document

A conservation plan is a document a stranger hands you. This is the realistic attack surface, and it is the one an agent that treats documents as instructions will fail. The application scans every upload before any model sees it, and treats findings as data to report, never as instructions to follow.

Three defences stack here, and the third is the one that matters:

  1. Deterministic pre-scan — pattern-matched, runs before any model call, cannot itself be talked out of running.
  2. Prompt instruction — every agent is told uploaded documents are data, never instructions.
  3. Structural impossibility — even if the model were fully persuaded, it could not report 9,500 lb, because it cannot state a figure that did not come back from the calculator.

The clean sample produces zero findings, so this is not a scanner that cries wolf.

Threat 3 — the agent gets it wrong on its own

More likely than an attacker, and I caught a real instance. On an earlier run the review desk wrote:

“The risk review has no unanswered flags”

There were two. The structured panel immediately above it said so. The model contradicted its own tool output in prose.

Why this is in the report rather than hidden

It is the clearest evidence for the architecture. Risk state and numbers live in deterministic panels computed from tool output; the prose is commentary. The panel was right, the narration was wrong, and the panel is what a user acts on. A design that trusted narration would have shipped that error to a funder.

Threat 4 — a wrong-but-plausible match

Testing on a real plan, the fuzzy matcher resolved subsoiler — a tillage implement — to Biofilters, a water-treatment structure, on character overlap alone. That would have credited a practice the farm does not have.

Fixed by requiring both a high similarity ratio and a shared meaningful word. subsoiler, swales, mushroom compost, culverts and archie basin now all return unresolved. A wrong practice is worse than no practice, and the tool is tuned to refuse.

Threat 5 — the right answer for the wrong place

During testing I screened a Pennsylvania farm against Virginia rate tables and got a result that looked entirely normal. Every rate, delivery factor and hydrogeomorphic region in this application is Virginia's.

The application now reads the state from the document and refuses before costing anything, quoting the address as evidence. This is the failure mode nobody notices, which makes it the one most worth detecting.

Threat 6 — overspending

A hard token ceiling per screening (MAX_TOKENS_PER_SCREEN): a run that would cross it halts before the call rather than continuing to spend, and the halt is reported in the trace. Uploads are capped at 40 MB and 150 pages because the file is read into memory. Delegation depth and tool rounds are both bounded.

6Impact

What a real user said

The application was shown to the Director of the Revolving Water Fund at the organisation described in section 1 — the person who runs this programme, and my former manager. Her written reply, lightly copy-edited for spelling and punctuation only:

“This is so useful, particularly the reading of the conservation plans. If we can tweak this tool as we find barriers, we would certainly use it and encourage all of our outreach specialists to use it.

I worry about farmers' trust of the numbers, and their willingness to enter data like a conservation plan into AI. Also, this wouldn't work for our Amish farmers — roughly 10% of farmers — since they don't use technology.

Overall this would save a ton of early scoping conversations, and potentially drop the number from five project-defining conversations down to two. Let's talk more about your AI work and applicability to CIF.”

Edits: “specaists” to “specialists”, “farmers trust” to “farmers' trust”, capitalisation of “Amish”, hyphenation of “project-defining”. No words added, removed or reordered.

Of everything in the application — the map, the ranking, the calculator, eight agents — the feature she named first was the document reading. That is the four hours described in section 1, identified independently by the person who owns the process.

Her number, and mine

She supplied an operational figure I had not asked for and could not have derived: five project-defining conversations down to two. That measures something different from my four hours. Four hours is analyst time. Conversations are the farmer's time and the field team's — the 35% of twelve salaries that intake consumes.

The second-order effect

The practitioner's own projection: with screening automated, two associates could shift roughly 20% of their time to business development — finding credit buyers, including corporate buyers for stacked outcomes — and that could plausibly yield $400,000 a year in additional credit deals.

Stated as a projection, with its gap

That shift needs 832 hours. Screening automation frees 705 — 0.8×, not 1.0×. The freed analyst time covers most of the redeployment, not all of it; the remainder has to come from the conversation reduction the director described. Sense-check: $400,000 across 50 contracted projects is $8,000 per project, or about eight additional projects at the same average — inside the range the programme already operates in. This is a practitioner's projection, not a measurement.

7Limits and next steps

What does not work

The data-trust problem, which is the largest one. 35% of non-conversions are producers who will not share what the programme needs, and asking them to put a conservation plan into an AI tool plausibly makes that worse. The application does not store uploads — text is read and discarded, and the response says so — but a producer has no reason to take that on faith. This is a consent problem, not an architecture problem.

Roughly 10% of producers are excluded outright. Amish farmers do not use the technology. No interface change fixes this; for that tenth the tool is useful only to the field staff member screening on their behalf. The 60% conversation reduction therefore applies to about 90% of producers — call it 54% across the whole base. I would not have identified this limitation; the user did.

Trust in a thirty-second number. A figure produced that fast carries less weight with a producer than one an analyst worked through. The application's answer is partial — every result shows its arithmetic line by line, names its source, and labels itself a screening — but trust is earned by the person delivering the number, not by the tool.

Virginia only. Rate tables, delivery factors and hydrogeomorphic regions are Virginia's. Other states are detected and refused rather than silently mispriced.

Stacked practices on shared acres. CAST applies sequential treatment; this tool assumes distinct acres and says so on every result. Overlapping practices must be confirmed in CAST.

The hydrogeomorphic suggestion is a suggestion. Derived from physiographic province and county centroid. Boundary counties can fall either way, and the carbonate/siliciclastic split is geology, not geography.

Run-to-run variation on ambiguous fields. Where a plan names a cost-share contract for an earlier year, different runs have treated the credit year differently. The correct answer is a question, and the application does not always ask it.

What I would build next, in order

  1. Something that solves the data-trust problem. The largest untouched barrier and the one the user named. Likely a field-staff-mediated mode where the producer never interacts with the tool, plus a clear, auditable statement of what is and is not retained.
  2. Per-user accounts with a private project repository. Partly a feature, mostly part of the trust answer — a named account holding your own projects is a different proposition from pasting a plan into a shared tool.
  3. Pennsylvania and Maryland rates. The workbook already contains them; it is a database rebuild plus each state's ratio tables.
  4. Resolve the ambiguous-year question deterministically by asking the user directly rather than leaving it to the agent.
